MMC-31

Discrete Mathematics
M.Sc. Mathematics · Semester III2017
  1. 1(a) Define validity of an argument form. State how an indirect proof of validity for a given argument form may be constructed. Hence construct an indirect proof of validity for the argument form \par \hspace*{2em}$(A\vee B)\to(C\vee D)$\par \hspace*{2em}$(C\vee E)\to(\sim F\wedge G)$\par \hspace*{2em}$(F\vee H)\to(A\wedge I)$\par \hspace*{2em}$\therefore\ \sim F$
  2. 1(b) Construct a proof of invalidity of the argument form \par \hspace*{2em}$A\vee(B\wedge C)$\par \hspace*{2em}$(A\vee B)\to(D\equiv\sim E)$\par \hspace*{2em}$(D\to\sim E)\to(E\wedge\sim F)$\par \hspace*{2em}$(F\to G)\wedge(G\to E)$\par \hspace*{2em}$(B\to C)\to G$\par \hspace*{2em}$\therefore\ G$ [4]
  3. 1(c) Symbolize the statement into an appropriate form:\par If something is lost, then if everyone values his possessions, then something will be missed. [2]
  4. 2(a) Construct a formal proof of validity for the argument form \par \hspace*{2em}$(\exists x)\left[U(x)\wedge(\forall y)(V(y)\to W(y))\right]$\par \hspace*{2em}$(\forall x)\left\{U(x)\to\left[(\exists y)(X(y)\wedge W(y))\to Y(x)\right]\right\}$\par \hspace*{2em}$\therefore\ (\exists y)(X(y)\wedge V(y))\to(\exists x)Y(x)$ [6]
  5. 2(b) Show that \par \hspace*{1em}i) $(\forall x)(Q\to F(x))\equiv\left[Q\to(\forall x)F(x)\right]$\par \hspace*{1em}ii) $(\forall x)(F(x)\to Q)\equiv\left[(\exists x)F(x)\to Q\right]$\par are valid forms. \par\hfill P.T.O.
  6. 3(a) Show that every graph $G$ with at least one edge has a subgraph $H$ with \par \hspace*{2em}$\delta(H)>\epsilon(H)\ge\epsilon(G)$ [2]
  7. 3(b) Show that every $n$-vertex graph with at least $n$ edges contains a cycle. Show also that a connected graph with $n$-vertices has exactly one cycle if and only if it has exactly $n$ edges.
  8. 3(c) In a connected graph $G$, an edge `$e$' is not a cyclic edge of $G$ if and only if `$e$' is a cut edge of $G$. [3]
  9. 4(a) Show that a connected graph is Eulerian if and only if its edge set can be decomposed into cycles. [3]
  10. 4(b) Show that a graph is a tree if and only if it is loop-free and has exactly one spanning tree. [3]
  11. 4(c) In a connected plane (simple) graph $G$ with $|E|>1$ and $|V|\ge3$, show that \par \hspace*{1em}i) $|E|\le3|V|-6$;\par \hspace*{1em}ii) there is a vertex $v$ of $G$ such that degree $(v)\le5$.
  12. 5(a) Count the number of regions into which a plane is sub-divided by drawing $n$ ovals on it such that each oval intersects the others at exactly two points. [4]
  13. 5(b) State Inclusion-Exclusion principle. Use it to count the integers in $\{1,2,\ldots,1000\}$ which are divisible by at least one of the primes 2, 3 or 5. [3]
  14. 5(c) In how many ways can the letters $\{a,a,a,a,a,b,b,b,b,c,c,c\}$ be arranged so that all letters of the same kind are not in a single block? [3]
  15. 6(a) State Pigeon-hole principle. Consider a sequence of $n^2+1$ distinct numbers. Use Pigeon-hole principle to show that there is a strictly monotone subsequence of at least $n+1$ of the numbers. Illustrate with an example of your choice of $n$.
  16. 6(b) The numbers 1 to 10 are arranged in random order around a circle. Show that there are three consecutive numbers whose sum is at least 17. [2]
  17. 6(c) Show that from 27 distinct positive odd numbers all less than 100, one can choose a pair of numbers whose sum is 102. [3]
